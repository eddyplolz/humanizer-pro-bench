# Hildegard of Bingen

**Hildegard of Bingen** (German: *Hildegard von Bingen*; Latin: *Hildegardis Bingensis*; 1098 – 17 September 1179), also known as **Saint Hildegard** and the **Sibyl of the Rhine**, was a German Benedictine abbess, writer, mystic, naturalist, and composer. She is one of the best-documented figures in the history of medieval music and one of the earliest composers whose biography and works are well preserved.

## Early life

Hildegard was born around 1098 in Bermersheim, in the Rhineland, to a noble family. According to tradition, she was the tenth child and was offered to the Church as a tithe. She was educated from about the age of eight at the Disibodenberg monastery, under the care of the anchoress Jutta of Sponheim. Hildegard reported experiencing visions from early childhood. After Jutta's death in 1136, she was elected leader of the women's community.

## Religious career

Around 1150, Hildegard moved her community to a new convent at Rupertsberg, near Bingen, and later founded a second house at Eibingen. Her visionary writings were reviewed and approved by Pope Eugenius III in 1147–48, which greatly enhanced her authority. She corresponded with popes, emperors, and other leaders, including Bernard of Clairvaux and Frederick Barbarossa, and undertook several preaching tours, which was unusual for a woman of her time.

## Musical works

Hildegard's surviving musical output exceeds that of any other medieval composer of chant. Around seventy-five compositions are extant, gathered chiefly in the *Dendermonde* and *Riesencodex* manuscripts. Her best-known work is the *Ordo Virtutum* ("Play of the Virtues"), a morality play set to music and one of the earliest surviving examples of liturgical drama. Her other major musical collection is the *Symphonia armonie celestium revelationum* ("Symphony of the Harmony of Celestial Revelations"), a cycle of antiphons, responsories, sequences, and hymns honoring saints, the Virgin Mary, and the Holy Spirit.

Her melodies are monophonic and notable for their wide range, soaring leaps, and expressive, melismatic lines, which differ from the narrower contours of typical Gregorian chant.

## Other writings

Hildegard's major visionary works include *Scivias* (c. 1151), *Liber vitae meritorum*, and *Liber divinorum operum*. She also wrote the natural-history and medical texts *Physica* and *Causae et curae*, and invented a constructed language, the *Lingua ignota*.

## Legacy

Hildegard died at Rupertsberg in 1179. Interest in her music revived greatly in the late 20th century, with numerous recordings reaching wide audiences. Although venerated as a saint for centuries, she was formally canonized by Pope Benedict XVI in 2012, who also named her a Doctor of the Church. Her feast day is 17 September.
