The **Battle of Hastings** was fought on **14 October 1066** between the forces of **William, Duke of Normandy**, and **King Harold Godwinson** of England. It was the निर्णायक battle of the **Norman Conquest of England** and led to the establishment of Norman rule. Although traditionally named after the nearby town of Hastings, the battle took place about 7 miles (11 km) to the northwest, near the present town of **Battle** in East Sussex.

The conflict arose from a disputed succession to the English throne. After the death of **Edward the Confessor** in January 1066, Harold Godwinson was crowned king. William of Normandy, however, claimed that Edward had earlier promised him the throne and that Harold had sworn to support his claim. A third claimant, **Harald Hardrada** of Norway, also invaded England. Harold Godwinson defeated and killed Hardrada at the **Battle of Stamford Bridge** on 25 September 1066, but the victory left his army exhausted.

Meanwhile, William crossed the English Channel and landed at **Pevensey** on 28 September 1066. He then marched into Sussex, where he built a prefabricated castle at Hastings and raided the surrounding countryside, partly to draw Harold south. Harold advanced rapidly from London with his household troops, the **housecarls**, and the levies of the shires, known as the **fyrd**.

The two armies met on **Senlac Hill**. Harold’s forces formed a dense **shield wall** along the ridge, while William’s army was composed of **infantry, archers, and cavalry**, reflecting Norman military practice. The battle lasted most of the day and was hard fought. Initial Norman assaults were repelled by the English line. At one stage, rumors spread that William had been killed, but he is said to have lifted his helmet to show that he was alive and rally his troops.

A crucial turning point came when elements of the English line broke formation, possibly in pursuit of retreating Normans. Whether these retreats were genuine or feigned remains debated by historians, but they allowed Norman cavalry to isolate and destroy parts of Harold’s army. Late in the day, Harold himself was killed, traditionally said to have been struck in the eye by an arrow, though the exact manner of his death is uncertain. With the king dead and much of the English nobility slain, resistance collapsed.

William’s victory opened the way to his coronation as **King William I of England** on **25 December 1066**. The battle transformed English society, aristocracy, language, and government, linking England more closely to continental Europe. It has been memorialized in sources such as the **Bayeux Tapestry**, one of the most important visual records of the conquest.
