# The Weight of Boxes

The first thing I noticed about my new city was how loud the silence was. In my old apartment, I knew every sound: the radiator's knock, the upstairs neighbor's heavy footsteps, the bus that groaned past at 6:40 each morning. Here, in a sublet that smelled faintly of someone else's cooking, I lay awake listening to noises I couldn't name.

Nobody warns you that moving is a kind of small, voluntary amnesia. You surrender your fluency. I had lived in my old city for nine years and could navigate it half asleep. I knew which grocery store stocked good tomatoes, which streets flooded in spring, which barista would start my order when I walked in. In the new city, I got lost walking to the pharmacy. I stood in the cereal aisle for ten minutes, undone by unfamiliar brands. It was humbling, and a little absurd, to feel like a child at thirty-four.

But the humbling is also the gift. Within a week, my attention sharpened in a way it hadn't in years. I noticed the color of the brick on my street, a warm rust that glowed at dusk. I noticed that people here nodded at strangers. I took the long way everywhere, because I had no short way yet. A city you don't know demands that you look at it, and looking, I realized, is a form of pleasure I'd stopped practicing. Routine had been quietly sanding down my days until they all felt the same.

There were smaller delights, too. I found a bakery where the owner began saving me the end pieces of the rye loaf. I discovered a river path where, on Sunday mornings, runners and old men with fishing rods shared the same few feet of asphalt in peaceful disregard. Each discovery felt like a private treasure, because no one had told me about it. I'd earned it by wandering.

Still, the costs were real, and they arrived on their own schedule. They came on a Tuesday, when something funny happened and I reached for my phone to tell a friend, then did the math on time zones and put it down. They came in the quiet of the second month, when the novelty thinned and I understood that friendship cannot be unpacked like a box. It accumulates slowly, through repetition and accident, through being around. I had traded people who knew my history for people who knew only my present, and that is a lonelier arrangement than I'd expected.

I've been here a year now. The bus no longer sounds foreign. I have a tomato place. Last month, a woman at the bakery asked how my week was going, and I realized I had a week worth describing. I still miss the old city, the way you miss a language you used to speak well.

But I've come to think that homesickness is simply the price of having loved a place, and that paying it is not a mistake. Moving taught me that I could be rebuilt, that I was more portable than I'd feared. The boxes are long gone. What I carried, it turns out, was never in them.
