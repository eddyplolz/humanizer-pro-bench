**Museum Returns Looted Artifacts to Country of Origin in Landmark Repatriation**

A major European museum has returned a collection of looted artifacts to their country of origin in a move being hailed as a significant step toward addressing the legacy of colonial-era plunder.

At a ceremony held on Tuesday, the museum formally handed over 27 objects to officials from Nigeria, including ceremonial sculptures, carved ivory pieces, and bronze plaques taken during a 19th-century military expedition. The artifacts, which had been housed in the museum’s African collections for more than a century, are expected to go on display in Lagos after conservation work is completed.

Museum director Elise Hartmann described the return as both “a moral responsibility and an institutional reckoning.”

“These objects were acquired in circumstances that were deeply unjust,” Hartmann said. “They are not simply works of art; they are part of a living cultural heritage. Returning them is the right thing to do.”

The repatriation follows years of pressure from Nigerian officials, scholars, and activists who have argued that artifacts seized during colonial campaigns should be restored to the communities from which they were taken. Recent provenance research conducted by the museum found that the objects were removed during a British punitive raid in 1897 and later sold through European art markets before entering the museum’s collection.

Nigeria’s Minister of Culture, Amina Bello, welcomed the return, calling it “an important act of justice, dignity, and historical acknowledgment.”

“For generations, these treasures were admired abroad while the people to whom they belong were denied access to them,” Bello said. “Their return does not erase the past, but it helps us confront it honestly and begin to repair the damage.”

The decision reflects a broader shift among museums in Europe and North America, many of which are reexamining the origins of items acquired during the colonial period. In recent years, several institutions have announced the return of bronzes, sacred objects, and human remains after evidence showed they had been taken through violence, coercion, or theft.

Still, the issue remains contentious. Some critics argue that museums serve a global public and should retain major collections while sharing them through loans and partnerships. Others say full repatriation is necessary to correct long-standing injustices and restore cultural sovereignty.

Experts say the latest return could encourage similar actions elsewhere. “This is not just about one museum or one set of objects,” said art historian Daniel Okoye. “It signals a growing recognition that possession without consent cannot be justified indefinitely.”

The museum said it plans to continue reviewing the provenance of hundreds of items in its collection and is in discussions with other countries regarding possible returns.

For many observers, Tuesday’s handover marked more than a transfer of property: it represented a public acknowledgment that history’s wounds can no longer remain behind glass.
