The **Atacama Desert** is a desert plateau in **South America**, located primarily in **northern Chile** and extending in places toward southern **Peru**. It is widely regarded as the **driest non-polar desert in the world**, with some weather stations in its interior having recorded no significant rainfall for many years. The desert lies between the **Pacific Ocean** to the west and the **Andes Mountains** to the east, forming a narrow north–south strip along the Pacific coast.

The Atacama covers roughly **105,000 square kilometres** (about 41,000 sq mi), though broader definitions include surrounding arid lands. Its extreme dryness is caused by a combination of factors: the **cold Humboldt Current** offshore suppresses evaporation and cloud formation, the **subtropical high-pressure system** inhibits precipitation, and the Andes block moist air from the Amazon Basin. As a result, much of the region receives only a few millimetres of rain annually, and some central areas may go decades without measurable precipitation.

Despite its harsh conditions, the Atacama supports specialized forms of life. Vegetation is sparse in the hyper-arid core, but cacti, shrubs, and hardy grasses occur in less extreme zones. Coastal fog, locally known as **camanchaca**, provides moisture that sustains some ecosystems. Animal life includes insects, reptiles, birds, and mammals adapted to aridity, while high-altitude wetlands and salt flats support flamingos and other Andean species.

The desert has long been inhabited by Indigenous peoples, including the **Atacameño** or **Likan Antai**, who developed settlements in oases and river valleys. European colonization brought the region under Spanish control, and in the 19th century the Atacama became economically important for its rich deposits of **nitrates**, **copper**, and other minerals. Territorial disputes over these resources contributed to the **War of the Pacific** (1879–1884), after which Chile gained control over extensive desert territories formerly held by Peru and Bolivia.

Today, the Atacama remains a major centre of **mining**, especially for copper and **lithium**. It is also internationally important for **astronomy**. The desert’s high elevation, minimal cloud cover, low humidity, and low levels of light pollution make it one of the best places on Earth for astronomical observation. Major observatories in or near the region include the **Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA)** and several facilities on **Cerro Paranal** and **La Silla**.

The Atacama is also a significant destination for **tourism** and scientific research. Its dramatic landscapes include salt flats, volcanoes, geysers, and eroded rock formations. Because of its dry, Mars-like conditions, the desert has been used as an analogue environment for testing space exploration technology and studying the limits of life in extreme environments.
