# Erie Canal

The **Erie Canal** is a historic canal in the U.S. state of New York that connects the Hudson River at Albany with Lake Erie at Buffalo. Completed in 1825, it was the first navigable waterway linking the Atlantic seaboard to the Great Lakes, and it played a decisive role in the economic development of the United States during the nineteenth century.

## History

Proposals for a canal across upstate New York circulated in the late eighteenth century, but the project gained momentum under the advocacy of Governor DeWitt Clinton, who secured state funding in 1817 after federal support failed to materialize. Critics derided the venture as "Clinton's Ditch." Construction began on July 4, 1817, at Rome, New York.

The canal was built largely by hand, using laborers, many of them Irish immigrants, along with horses, oxen, and simple tools. Engineers with little formal training, such as Benjamin Wright and James Geddes, learned on the job, devising methods for clearing forests, stumps, and swampland. The original channel was about 363 miles (584 km) long, 40 feet (12 m) wide, and 4 feet (1.2 m) deep, with 83 locks overcoming an elevation difference of roughly 565 feet (172 m).

The canal officially opened on October 26, 1825, when Clinton traveled from Buffalo to New York City in a ceremonial voyage, symbolically pouring Lake Erie water into the Atlantic Ocean in the "Wedding of the Waters."

## Economic and social impact

The canal dramatically reduced the cost and time of moving freight. Shipping a ton of goods from Buffalo to New York City fell from around $100 to roughly $10, and travel time dropped from weeks to days. Agricultural products from the Midwest flowed east, while manufactured goods and settlers moved west.

New York City surpassed rival ports such as Philadelphia and Boston to become the nation's leading commercial center. Cities along the route, including Syracuse, Rochester, Utica, and Buffalo, grew rapidly. The corridor also became a center of social change, associated with religious revivalism, the women's rights movement, and abolitionism, leading some historians to call western New York the "burned-over district."

## Enlargement and modern use

The canal was enlarged between 1836 and 1862, and from 1905 to 1918 it was replaced by the larger **New York State Barge Canal**, which incorporated rivers and lakes into its route. Railroads and later highways and the St. Lawrence Seaway reduced commercial traffic in the twentieth century.

Today the canal is used primarily for recreation, including boating, cycling, and tourism along the Erie Canalway Trail. In 2000, Congress designated the Erie Canalway National Heritage Corridor to preserve its legacy. The canal remains operated by the New York State Canal Corporation.
