# Dust Bowl

The **Dust Bowl**, also known as the **Dirty Thirties**, was a period of severe dust storms that caused major ecological and agricultural damage to the American and Canadian prairie lands during the 1930s. The phenomenon was caused by severe drought combined with decades of extensive farming practices that did not include techniques to prevent wind erosion, such as crop rotation, cover crops, and terracing.

## Background

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, settlers moved onto the Great Plains in large numbers, encouraged by the Homestead Acts and by unusually wet weather in the early 1900s. The native deep-rooted prairie grasses were plowed under to plant wheat. High wheat prices during and after World War I accelerated this expansion, and the introduction of gasoline-powered tractors and combines allowed farmers to cultivate far more land than before. Millions of acres of grassland were converted to cropland, leaving the soil exposed.

## Drought and storms

A series of droughts began in 1930 and recurred in waves through 1936, 1939–1940. With the grasses that once anchored the soil gone, high winds lifted the dry topsoil into enormous clouds. The most severe region included the panhandles of Texas and Oklahoma, as well as parts of western Kansas, eastern Colorado, and northeastern New Mexico.

On **April 14, 1935**, remembered as "Black Sunday," a massive storm carried dust across the southern plains, turning day into night. The following day, journalist Robert Geiger used the term "Dust Bowl" in a news dispatch, and the name endured. Some storms carried dust as far as Washington, D.C., and ships in the Atlantic Ocean.

## Human impact

The storms damaged crops, buried farm equipment, and killed livestock. Many residents developed "dust pneumonia" and other respiratory illnesses. Combined with the economic hardship of the Great Depression, the crisis forced many families to leave their farms. Roughly 3.5 million people moved out of the Plains states in the 1930s, with many heading to California. Their experiences were chronicled in works such as John Steinbeck's novel *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939), Dorothea Lange's photographs, and Woody Guthrie's songs.

## Government response

The federal government established programs to address the crisis. The Soil Conservation Service, created in 1935 under Hugh Hammond Bennett, promoted soil-saving techniques including contour plowing, strip cropping, and crop rotation. The Prairie States Forestry Project, begun in 1934, planted millions of trees in windbreaks, known as shelterbelts, across the Plains. The Taylor Grazing Act of 1934 also regulated grazing on public lands.

## Legacy

Rainfall returned in late 1939, ending the drought. The Dust Bowl remains one of the worst environmental disasters in American history and shaped modern soil conservation policy and agricultural practices.
