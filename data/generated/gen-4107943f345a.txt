# History of printing in Korea

The **history of printing in Korea** spans more than a thousand years and includes some of the earliest known examples of woodblock printing and metal movable-type printing. Korean printing developed largely within Buddhist and state institutions, and it was central to the preservation of religious, historical, and administrative texts.

## Early woodblock printing

Woodblock printing reached Korea through contact with Tang China. The *Mugujeonggwang Great Dharani Sutra*, discovered in 1966 inside a pagoda at Bulguksa temple, is generally dated to before 751 in the Unified Silla period. It is among the oldest surviving printed texts in the world.

During the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392), printing flourished under royal and monastic patronage. The *Tripitaka Koreana*, a complete Buddhist canon, was carved on more than 80,000 woodblocks between 1236 and 1251, partly as an act of devotion during the Mongol invasions. The blocks are preserved at Haeinsa temple and were inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World Register in 2007.

## Metal movable type

Goryeo printers developed movable type cast in metal, probably in the early 13th century. Records suggest that the *Sangjeong Gogeum Yemun* was printed with metal type around 1234, though no copy survives. The oldest extant book printed with movable metal type is the *Jikji*, a collection of Zen Buddhist teachings printed at Heungdeoksa temple in Cheongju in 1377. Its second volume is held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France and was added to the Memory of the World Register in 2001.

## Joseon dynasty

The early Joseon state made printing a government enterprise. King Taejong founded the Type Foundry Office (*Jujaso*) in 1403, and several sets of bronze type followed, including the *gyemi* type (1403), the *gyeongja* type (1420), and the *gabin* type (1434) under King Sejong. These fonts were used to print Confucian classics, histories, agricultural manuals, and the *Hunminjeongeum* (1446), which introduced the Korean alphabet, Hangul.

Printing was mostly limited to government offices and elite circles, and commercial publishing remained small compared with later developments in Europe. The Japanese invasions of 1592–1598 destroyed or removed many type sets, and later rulers often relied on wooden or recast type.

## Modern period

Western-style printing technology arrived in the late 19th century. The *Hansŏng Sunbo*, launched in 1883 by a government office, is regarded as Korea's first modern newspaper. In 1896 *The Independent* (*Tongnip Sinmun*) became the first privately run newspaper to use Hangul extensively. Printing expanded rapidly in the 20th century, though it was heavily restricted during the Japanese colonial period (1910–1945).

## Legacy

Korea's early use of metal type is a point of national pride and scholarly interest. Historians generally regard it as independent of Johannes Gutenberg's later invention in Europe, and no direct transmission has been demonstrated. Today the Cheongju Early Printing Museum and the Haeinsa woodblock repository preserve this heritage.
