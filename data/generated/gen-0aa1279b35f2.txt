# The Friendship That Lives in My Pocket

When I was twelve, my best friend moved across the country, and we wrote letters. They were clumsy, full of crossed-out words and drawings in the margins. A reply took two weeks, sometimes three. I remember the small thrill of seeing her handwriting in the mailbox, and how I read each letter so many times that the folds went soft. We lost touch within a year. Back then, distance simply did that.

My own kids will never know that kind of loss, and I'm still deciding how I feel about it.

Social media has rearranged friendship in ways I notice most when I try to explain them. I know what my college roommate had for dinner last night. I know her daughter lost a tooth, that her mother has been ill, that she's frustrated with her job. I haven't spoken to her in four years. Is she my friend? By the old definition, probably not. By the new one, she's someone I "keep up with," which is its own strange category, closeness without contact, intimacy without exchange.

There are real gifts in this. I've watched friendships survive moves, career changes, and the ordinary drift that once ended them. A friend in another time zone sends me voice memos while she walks her dog, and I answer while I do dishes. It's not the same as sitting across from her, but it isn't nothing. It's a thread, and threads can be pulled tight again. When we finally see each other, we don't start from zero.

But the costs are quieter, and I've felt them more as I've gotten older. Social media makes friendship feel like a performance with an audience. I've caught myself composing a moment while I'm still inside it, angling the dinner table for a photo, thinking of a caption while a friend is mid-sentence. The experience becomes content, and the friend becomes, in some small way, a co-star.

It has also made affection cheap. A like costs nothing. A birthday message from a platform's reminder takes four seconds. We send these little tokens and feel we've done the work of caring, when real friendship has always required something less efficient: showing up, sitting in awkward silence, driving across town when someone's life falls apart. No app has automated that, though it has made us feel as if we don't need to.

Last winter, a friend I'd only ever "liked" from afar posted something vague and sad. I almost scrolled past. Instead, I called her. We talked for two hours, and she cried, and I realized how long it had been since I'd heard her actual voice. The post had been a small flare, and I'd nearly mistaken it for scenery.

That's where I've landed. Social media didn't ruin friendship or perfect it. It widened the net and thinned the rope. It's up to us to decide what to do with the slack, whether to treat the feed as the friendship itself or as a doorway into one.

I still have my friend's old letters, soft at the folds. I don't want to go back to waiting three weeks for a reply. But I'd like to write like that again: slowly, with crossed-out words, meaning every one.
