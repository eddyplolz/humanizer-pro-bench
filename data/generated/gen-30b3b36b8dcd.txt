# Judge Blocks $1.2 Billion Highway Expansion, Citing Flawed Environmental Review

**RIVERTON —** A state court judge on Tuesday halted a planned $1.2 billion expansion of Interstate 74, ruling that transportation officials failed to adequately study the project's effects on air quality and nearby wetlands.

In a 58-page decision, Judge Marianne Holloway of the Franklin County Superior Court found that the State Department of Transportation relied on outdated traffic projections and did not meaningfully consider alternatives to widening the 22-mile corridor from four lanes to eight.

"The agency's analysis treated its preferred outcome as a foregone conclusion," Holloway wrote. "The law requires a hard look at the consequences, not a justification after the fact."

The ruling grants an injunction that prohibits construction, land acquisition, and contractor awards until the department completes a new environmental impact statement. Work was scheduled to begin in March.

The lawsuit was filed last year by a coalition of residents, environmental groups, and the town of Millbrook, which argued the expansion would increase pollution near schools and destroy 140 acres of protected wetlands along the Tallow River.

"This is a victory for communities that were told their concerns didn't matter," said Dana Ortiz, a Millbrook resident and lead plaintiff. "We never opposed improving safety. We opposed a plan that ignored the evidence."

Transportation Secretary Glenn Whitaker said the department was disappointed and is weighing an appeal. "This corridor sees more than 90,000 vehicles a day and has one of the highest crash rates in the state," he said in a statement. "Delay carries real costs, both in safety and in dollars."

The department estimates that a year's delay could add $60 million to the project's price tag. Business groups, including the Riverton Chamber of Commerce, criticized the decision, saying the expansion was essential to relieving freight bottlenecks.

"Every day that road stays congested, we lose productivity and jobs," said chamber president Alan Brooks.

Opponents countered that the department's own data showed congestion would return within a decade of completion, a phenomenon researchers call induced demand. The judge noted this concern, stating that the agency "dismissed without analysis" studies suggesting wider highways often attract additional traffic.

Legal observers said the department has several options: appeal, revise the study, or consider alternatives such as added transit service, targeted safety upgrades, or a smaller expansion.

"A new review could take 18 months or longer," said Priya Natarajan, a land-use law professor at Riverton State University. "But if it's done thoroughly, it may produce a project that survives challenge."

The department has 30 days to file an appeal.
