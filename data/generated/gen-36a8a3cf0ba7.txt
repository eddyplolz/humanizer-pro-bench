# The Teacher Who Waited

In seventh grade, I had a math teacher named Mr. Okafor who did something I found infuriating at the time: he refused to give me the answer.

I was stuck on a word problem about two trains, and I raised my hand with the confidence of someone who believed adults existed to resolve confusion quickly. He walked over, looked at my paper, and asked, "What do you know so far?" I said I knew nothing. He said, "Great. Let's write that down." Then he waited. Not impatiently, not with a sigh, but with the calm of a man with all afternoon. Eventually, to fill the silence, I began listing things: the trains left at different times, one was faster. Somewhere in the middle of my own sentence, I saw it.

I've thought about that silence for years. It taught me that a good teacher understands something counterintuitive: the goal isn't to transfer knowledge but to build the person who can find it.

Of course, knowing the material matters. A teacher who doesn't understand the subject can't guide anyone through it. But I've met brilliant people who were terrible teachers, because they'd forgotten what it felt like not to know. Expertise can make you impatient with confusion. Mr. Okafor seemed to treat confusion as a natural, even interesting, place to stand. He never made me feel stupid for being there.

That, I think, is the second quality: a good teacher makes it safe to be wrong. In his classroom, mistakes went on the board as public property, examined with curiosity rather than judgment. "Interesting," he'd say. "Where did this go sideways?" We learned to hunt our own errors like detectives rather than hide them like evidence. I've carried that into adulthood, where admitting a mistake early is often the difference between a small problem and a catastrophic one.

The third quality is harder to describe. Good teachers believe in you slightly before you've earned it. Mr. Okafor once handed me a problem from a competition packet meant for older students and said, "I think you'd enjoy this one." I didn't solve it. But I spent a week trying, and I remember the feeling of being taken seriously. His expectation was a kind of loan, and I worked hard to repay it.

I've since had teachers who were more charismatic, more entertaining, more celebrated. Some were wonderful. But when I think about who changed how I think, I return to the man who stood beside my desk and let a silence stretch until I filled it myself.

It takes a particular kind of restraint to teach this way. It would have been faster to show me the solution, and I'd have been grateful for about ten minutes. Instead he gave me something slower and more durable: the experience of figuring it out, and the quiet discovery that I could.

I don't remember the answer to the train problem. I remember the waiting. I think that's the point.
