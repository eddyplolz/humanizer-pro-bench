**History of the bicycle**

The **history of the bicycle** traces the development of a human-powered vehicle that became one of the most widespread forms of personal transport in the world. From early experimental designs in the early 19th century, the bicycle evolved through major mechanical innovations in steering, pedals, chain drive, and pneumatic tires, eventually becoming both a practical means of transportation and a popular sporting and recreational device.

The earliest widely recognized predecessor of the bicycle was the **dandy horse**, also known as the **draisine**, invented by the German baron **Karl von Drais** in 1817. Constructed largely of wood, it had two in-line wheels and a steerable front wheel, but no pedals. Riders propelled it by pushing their feet against the ground. Drais’s invention demonstrated the basic balance-and-steer principle that would underpin later bicycle design.

In the 1860s, inventors in France developed the **velocipede**, sometimes called the **boneshaker** because of its rigid wooden or metal frame and iron-banded wheels, which made for an uncomfortable ride on rough roads. These machines introduced pedals mounted directly to the front wheel, allowing continuous propulsion without touching the ground. Pierre Michaux and Pierre Lallement are among those often associated with the velocipede’s development and commercialization.

During the 1870s, the **high-wheel bicycle**, or **penny-farthing**, became a prominent form. Its very large front wheel enabled greater speed because the pedals were fixed directly to that wheel; a larger diameter increased distance traveled per pedal revolution. However, the design was difficult to mount and dangerous, as riders sat high above the ground and were vulnerable to forward falls, sometimes called “headers.”

A major breakthrough came in the 1880s with the emergence of the **safety bicycle**. This design, associated especially with **John Kemp Starley** and his 1885 **Rover**, used two wheels of similar size, a chain drive to the rear wheel, and a lower center of gravity. It was much safer and more accessible than the penny-farthing. The safety bicycle established the basic form of the modern bicycle.

Another crucial innovation was the **pneumatic tire**, patented by **John Boyd Dunlop** in 1888. Air-filled tires greatly improved comfort and speed by absorbing shocks from uneven road surfaces. Combined with advances such as diamond-frame construction, ball bearings, and improved brakes, the pneumatic tire helped transform the bicycle into a practical vehicle for everyday use.

By the late 19th century, bicycles had become central to social and economic life in Europe and North America. They expanded personal mobility, influenced road improvement movements, and played a notable role in women’s emancipation by offering independent transportation and encouraging changes in clothing and social conventions. In the 20th century, bicycles remained important for transport, sport, and industry, and in the 21st century they have gained renewed significance in urban mobility, environmental policy, and public health.
