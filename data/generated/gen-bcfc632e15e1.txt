# What the Walls Remember

When I was nine, my grandmother took me to the train station in the town where she grew up. It had been closed for decades, its windows clouded and its ticket counter long since stripped of brass. But she pressed her palm flat against the wall of the waiting room as though checking a sleeping animal for breath. "I left for the city from this bench," she said. "Your grandfather proposed on that platform." The building was nothing special by any architect's measure. To her, it was a hinge on which her whole life had swung.

I think about that moment whenever someone argues that old buildings are simply obstacles, expensive to maintain and inconvenient to retrofit, standing where something shinier could go. The argument isn't foolish. Old buildings leak. They resist wiring. They often fail to meet modern codes, and sentiment alone won't pay for a new roof. I don't believe every old structure deserves rescue. A building isn't sacred merely for surviving.

But some deserve it, and I've come to believe the reasons are practical as well as tender.

First, old buildings are a form of memory that no archive can replicate. A photograph shows you how a street looked; a building lets you stand inside the proportions people once considered normal. You feel how low the ceilings were, how narrow the stairs, how much the light depended on windows rather than switches. History stops being an abstraction when you have to duck through its doorway.

Second, they carry craftsmanship we've mostly stopped affording. In the library of my hometown, a century-old building I used to haunt as a teenager, someone had carved tiny acorns into the banister. No one required this. No budget meeting would approve it today. Yet generations of restless kids ran their hands over those acorns, and I suspect it taught us, without anyone saying so, that ordinary places could be made with care.

Third, there's the quiet argument of continuity. Neighborhoods made entirely of new construction can feel oddly weightless, like a stage set waiting for a story. Old buildings give a place its sense of having been lived in. They let a newcomer and a lifelong resident share a landmark, even if they remember it differently. That shared reference point is a thread in the social fabric, and fabric is easier to tear than to weave.

Finally, there's the plain matter of waste. The greenest building, as preservationists like to say, is often the one already standing. Demolition discards the energy and materials embedded in it, and fills landfills besides.

The train station was eventually torn down for a parking lot. Last year I drove through town and tried to find the spot where the bench had been. I couldn't. The asphalt was smooth and the painted lines were crisp, and nothing indicated that a proposal, or a departure, had ever happened there.

That's what I'd want the skeptics to understand. We don't preserve buildings because stone and timber are precious. We preserve them because they hold the places where our lives took their shape, and once they're gone, so is the bench, the palm against the wall, and the proof.
