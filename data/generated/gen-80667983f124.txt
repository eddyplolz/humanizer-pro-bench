By Thursday afternoon, I often notice a strange shift in the office. People are still answering emails, attending meetings, and updating spreadsheets, but the energy has changed. The sharpness of Monday is gone. Conversations drift. Small tasks take longer than they should. By Friday, it sometimes feels as if everyone is performing the idea of work more than truly doing it. That feeling has made me increasingly sympathetic to the case for a four-day work week.

What persuades me most is not the fantasy of having one more day to sleep in, though that is certainly appealing. It is the growing recognition that more time at work does not always mean better work. In many jobs, especially office jobs, productivity is not a simple matter of hours logged. Attention is finite. Creativity fades when it is overused. Decision-making becomes sloppier when people are tired. We often talk as if endurance were the highest professional virtue, but in practice, clarity matters more than sheer duration. A shorter week acknowledges something deeply human: that people are not machines, and pretending otherwise usually leads to mediocrity.

I also find the four-day week compelling because it reframes rest as something valuable rather than indulgent. In many work cultures, exhaustion is worn almost as a badge of honor. To be overwhelmed is to be important; to be unavailable is to be needed. But this logic feels increasingly hollow. Rest is not the opposite of responsibility. It is one of the conditions that make responsibility possible. An extra day away from work can mean time to recover, to care for children or parents, to make a doctor’s appointment without stress, to read, to exercise, to exist outside the demands of employers and inboxes. When people have room to live fuller lives, they often return to work more focused and less resentful.

There is also, I think, a moral argument for the four-day work week. For generations, technological advances have been sold as pathways to efficiency and freedom. Yet for many workers, those efficiencies have not translated into more leisure, only into higher expectations. We can answer messages instantly, produce documents faster, and automate routine tasks, but the reward for doing so is often simply more work. A four-day week offers a different possibility: that the benefits of innovation might finally be shared in the form of time. Time is, after all, one of the most precious resources any person has.

Of course, not every profession can adopt the same schedule in the same way. Hospitals, schools, factories, and service industries all have practical constraints. A four-day work week is not a magical fix for every workplace problem. It requires thoughtful implementation, staffing adjustments, and an honest commitment not to cram five days of meetings into four. But the existence of challenges does not weaken the central idea. It strengthens the need to rethink old assumptions.

To me, the case for a four-day work week is ultimately a case for dignity. It asks whether work should consume the best of our lives, or support them. I do not think people want to contribute less. I think they want to contribute sustainably, and still have enough of themselves left over for the rest of life.
