# Great Molasses Flood

The **Great Molasses Flood**, also known as the **Boston Molasses Disaster**, occurred on January 15, 1919, in the North End neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts. A large storage tank filled with molasses burst, sending a wave of molasses through the streets. The flood killed 21 people and injured about 150.

## Background

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of the United States Industrial Alcohol Company (USIA). It stood about 50 feet (15 m) tall and 90 feet (27 m) in diameter, and held roughly 2.3 million US gallons of molasses. The molasses was brought by ship from the Caribbean and fermented into industrial alcohol, which was used in the manufacture of munitions. It was also used in the production of rum.

The tank was completed in 1915 and was poorly built. The steel was thinner than the tank's size required, and it was never properly tested by being filled with water. It leaked from the start, and the company reportedly painted it brown to hide the leakage rather than fix the problem. Residents collected leaking molasses from the base of the tank.

## Disaster

On the day of the disaster, temperatures in Boston had risen sharply, from about 2 °F (−17 °C) to above 40 °F (4 °C). Shortly after noon, the tank ruptured. Witnesses described a loud rumbling and the sound of rivets popping like machine-gun fire. A wave of molasses estimated at up to 25 feet (7.6 m) high at its crest moved through the streets at around 35 miles per hour (56 km/h).

The wave destroyed buildings, damaged the elevated railway structure, and swept up people, horses, and vehicles. Most of the dead were killed by drowning or by injuries from debris. Rescue was difficult because the thick, cooling molasses trapped victims.

## Aftermath

Boston firefighters, police, Red Cross workers, and sailors from the USS *Nantucket* took part in rescue and cleanup efforts. Cleanup took weeks, and the harbor remained brown with molasses into the summer.

Residents filed one of the earliest major class-action lawsuits in Massachusetts against USIA. The company claimed that anarchists had sabotaged the tank, but an auditor appointed by the court, Hugh W. Ogden, concluded after hearing years of testimony that the tank's structural design was inadequate. In 1925, USIA was ordered to pay damages, and settlements totaled roughly $600,000. The case contributed to stricter building regulations and to the requirement that engineers and architects sign off on construction plans.

## Legacy

A plaque commemorates the disaster near the site, now a park and playground. A popular legend holds that the neighborhood smells of molasses on hot summer days. Later scientific studies, including research presented in 2016 by Harvard-affiliated researchers, have examined how the cold temperature affected the molasses's viscosity and made the flood especially deadly.
