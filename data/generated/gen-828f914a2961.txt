Boredom has an unfair reputation. We speak of it as if it were a minor curse, something to be avoided with the same urgency as hunger or loneliness. A boring class, a boring conversation, a boring afternoon—these feel like failures of design, evidence that life is not entertaining us as it should. For a long time, I agreed. If I found myself waiting in line, sitting on a bus, or staring at the ceiling with nothing immediate to do, I reached for my phone almost automatically. I treated every empty space in the day as a problem to solve. Only later did I begin to understand that boredom was not always an emptiness. Sometimes it was an opening.

As a child, I encountered boredom in its purest form during summer afternoons. There were hours when the heat was too heavy for running outside, the television offered nothing appealing, and my friends were away. At first, those afternoons felt unbearable. I remember pacing from room to room, announcing to anyone who would listen that there was “nothing to do.” But after the complaint had exhausted itself, something curious often happened. I would begin building strange worlds out of ordinary things: blankets became forts, notebooks became newspapers for imaginary cities, and a box of mismatched buttons became treasure. Boredom was the threshold I had to cross before imagination could get to work.

That pattern did not disappear when I grew older; it merely became harder to notice. Adult life offers endless escapes from boredom, many of them glowing in the palm of the hand. The moment a task becomes repetitive or a silence begins to stretch, distraction is available. Yet I have found that constant stimulation leaves me feeling oddly thin, as though my attention has been broken into so many pieces that none of them can settle anywhere. Boredom, by contrast, has a way of gathering me back together. It slows time enough for thoughts I have been avoiding—or too busy to hear—to rise to the surface.

Some of my clearest ideas have arrived in boring moments: while folding laundry, walking a familiar route, or sitting through the first uneventful minutes of a delayed train. In those spaces, my mind starts wandering, and wandering is not the same thing as wasting time. It is often the beginning of reflection. Boredom strips away urgency and invites a different kind of attention, one less efficient but more spacious. It allows memory, curiosity, and even discomfort to speak. Without it, I suspect I would know less about what I truly think.

There is also a moral value to boredom. To be bored is to confront the fact that not every moment exists for our amusement. The world does not owe us constant novelty. Learning to endure tedium—to sit still in a waiting room, to listen through a quiet pause, to let an afternoon unfold without demanding instant gratification—can be a form of patience. It teaches humility. It reminds us that a meaningful life is not composed only of thrilling scenes, but also of ordinary stretches that ask for endurance, presence, and invention.

I still dislike boredom sometimes. I still reach for distraction more often than I mean to. But I no longer see boredom as a void to be filled as quickly as possible. I see it as a necessary interval, a pause in which something subtler can emerge. In a culture that fears silence and worships stimulation, boredom may be one of the last places where the mind can hear itself think.
