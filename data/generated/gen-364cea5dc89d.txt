The **Great Molasses Flood**, also known as the **Boston Molasses Disaster**, was an industrial accident that occurred on **January 15, 1919**, in the **North End neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts**. A large storage tank owned by the **United States Industrial Alcohol Company** burst, releasing a massive wave of molasses into the surrounding streets. The disaster killed **21 people** and injured around **150 others**, and it remains one of the most unusual and destructive urban accidents in American history.

The storage tank, located near **Commercial Street**, stood about 50 feet (15 m) high and 90 feet (27 m) in diameter. It contained approximately **2.3 million gallons** (8.7 million liters) of molasses, which was used to produce industrial alcohol, an important component in the manufacture of munitions during and after World War I. On the day of the disaster, the tank suddenly ruptured, sending a fast-moving surge of molasses through the neighborhood. Contemporary reports described the wave as being up to **25 feet** (7.6 m) high and moving at an estimated **35 miles per hour** (56 km/h), though exact figures remain debated.

The flood caused widespread devastation. Buildings were knocked from their foundations, elevated railway supports were damaged, and horses, wagons, and people were trapped in the thick, sticky liquid. Rescue efforts were hampered by the unusual nature of the disaster, as molasses coated streets, debris, and victims, making movement extremely difficult. Firefighters, police officers, soldiers, and local residents took part in the rescue and cleanup. The area remained coated in syrup for weeks, and accounts later claimed that Boston residents could smell molasses on warm days for years afterward.

In the aftermath, numerous lawsuits were filed against the company. A lengthy legal process followed, and investigators concluded that the tank had been poorly constructed and inadequately tested. Evidence suggested that the steel walls were too thin and that the structure had suffered from leaks even before the collapse. The disaster became an important early example of corporate liability and helped establish more rigorous standards for engineering oversight, construction, and industrial safety in the United States.

The Great Molasses Flood has since become a well-known episode in Boston history and American popular memory. It has been the subject of books, documentaries, and local folklore, often remembered both for its tragic loss of life and for the bizarre circumstances of the event. Historians view it as a significant case study in industrial negligence, urban disaster response, and the legal evolution of accountability in the early 20th century.
