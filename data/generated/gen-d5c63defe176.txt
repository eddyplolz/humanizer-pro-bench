**History of the printing press in Korea**

The history of the printing press in Korea is notable for its early development of both woodblock and metal movable type printing, which played an important role in the transmission of Buddhism, Confucian learning, and government administration. Korean printing technology emerged centuries before the European movable-type press and is regarded as one of the major achievements in the history of East Asian book culture.

Printing in Korea began with **woodblock printing**, which was introduced from China and was in use by the Unified Silla period. One of the earliest surviving examples is the *Great Dharani Sutra*, discovered at Bulguksa Temple and generally dated to the 8th century. During the **Goryeo dynasty** (918–1392), woodblock printing expanded significantly, especially in connection with Buddhist texts. The most famous product of this tradition is the **Tripitaka Koreana**, a complete collection of Buddhist scriptures carved onto more than 80,000 wooden blocks in the 13th century. Created in part as an act of devotion and national protection during the Mongol invasions, it remains one of the most important achievements of premodern printing.

Korea is especially renowned for the invention and use of **metal movable type**. Although the precise origins are debated, Korean artisans had developed movable metal type by the early 13th century, well before Johannes Gutenberg’s press in 15th-century Europe. The technology was supported by the Goryeo court, which sought efficient methods for producing official and scholarly texts. The oldest extant book printed with metal movable type is **Jikji**, a Buddhist anthology printed in 1377 at Heungdeok Temple in Cheongju. *Jikji* predates Gutenberg’s *42-line Bible* by several decades and is recognized by UNESCO for its historical significance.

Under the **Joseon dynasty** (1392–1897), printing became closely tied to the needs of the Confucian state. The government established official printing offices and sponsored the casting of new fonts of metal type. Printed books were used to disseminate legal codes, histories, agricultural manuals, and Confucian classics. The invention of **Hangul** in the 15th century during the reign of King Sejong further influenced Korean print culture by eventually making written materials more accessible, although Classical Chinese remained dominant in elite publications for centuries.

Despite these innovations, Korean printing did not produce a social transformation identical to that associated with print in early modern Europe. Literacy remained limited, and printing was largely controlled by the court, aristocracy, temples, and scholarly institutions. Nevertheless, Korea’s contributions to the history of printing are substantial. Its early mastery of woodblock production and pioneering development of metal movable type place Korea among the most important centers in the global history of the press.
