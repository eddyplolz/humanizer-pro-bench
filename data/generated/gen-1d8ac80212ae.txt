**Public-Transit Workers Launch Strike, Disrupting Commutes Across the City**

Thousands of commuters faced major delays and disruptions Tuesday after public-transit workers walked off the job in a strike that halted most bus, subway, and tram services across the city.

The strike began shortly after midnight, following weeks of unsuccessful contract negotiations between the Transit Workers Union and the Metropolitan Transit Authority. Union leaders say workers are demanding higher wages, stronger job protections, and improved staffing levels, arguing that employees have endured years of mounting pressure amid rising living costs and persistent labor shortages.

“This strike is a last resort,” said union president Maria Delgado at an early morning rally outside the transit authority’s headquarters. “Our members keep this city moving every day, but they are being asked to do more with less. We need a contract that reflects the value of their work and guarantees safe, reliable service for the public.”

Transit officials said they were disappointed by the walkout and warned that the strike could have severe economic consequences if it continues for several days. In a statement, the authority said it had offered a “fair and responsible” contract package that included pay increases and benefit protections, but claimed the union’s demands would place an unsustainable burden on the transit system’s already strained budget.

“We remain committed to reaching an agreement as quickly as possible,” said transit authority chief executive Daniel Mercer. “We urge union leadership to return to the bargaining table so we can restore service for the millions who depend on public transportation.”

By dawn, commuters crowded rideshare pick-up zones, packed into limited regional rail services, and formed long lines at bike-share stations. Downtown streets were heavily congested as more residents turned to cars, taxis, and carpooling to get to work. Several schools reported higher-than-usual absentee rates, while some businesses said employees were arriving late or working remotely where possible.

City officials urged residents to avoid nonessential travel and announced temporary measures to ease congestion, including expanded bike lanes, additional traffic officers at major intersections, and increased commuter-rail capacity. The mayor’s office also said it was monitoring the situation closely and encouraging both sides to resume talks with the help of a state-appointed mediator.

For many riders, the strike highlighted the central role public transit plays in everyday life. “I understand why workers are upset, but this is chaos,” said commuter Jasmine Lee, who spent nearly two hours trying to reach her office. “So many people rely on these trains and buses. I just hope they settle this soon.”

Negotiations were expected to continue Tuesday afternoon, though neither side indicated how close they were to a deal. Until an agreement is reached, city residents are bracing for continued disruption and an uncertain commute.
