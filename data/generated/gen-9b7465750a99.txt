# The Page That Listens

In the back of my closet, in a shoebox that once held winter boots, there are eleven notebooks. Some have cracked spines and coffee rings. One has a lock whose key I lost in 2009. I haven't read most of them since I wrote them, and yet I can't imagine throwing them away. This puzzles me, because if I never reread them, what exactly are they for?

I've come to think the answer is that a journal is less about the future reader than the present writer. When I'm tangled in a worry, it exists as a fog, shapeless and large. But to write it down, I must choose words. I must decide what actually happened, what I actually feel, and which of those two things is bothering me. The fog becomes a sentence, and a sentence can be looked at. Half the time, by the end of the page, the problem has shrunk to its true size. The other half, it has grown, and I finally understand why I've been so tired.

People keep journals, I suspect, because we are all desperate for a witness. Most of our days vanish without being seen: the small humiliation in a meeting, the odd joy of a light-filled kitchen, the thought that arrived at a red light and then evaporated. Friends can't hear everything, and shouldn't have to. A journal asks nothing in return. It doesn't get bored or give advice or turn the conversation toward itself. It receives, and being received is a quiet kind of relief.

There's also the matter of time. Memory is a notoriously unreliable editor. It smooths rough years into tidy stories, and it forgets how uncertain we were while living them. Last spring I opened an old notebook and found myself, at twenty-six, convinced that a certain job rejection had ruined my life. I had no memory of that despair. I only remembered the better thing that followed. Reading it, I felt tenderness for that anxious person, along with a small, useful humility: the things I'm sure are catastrophes today may be tomorrow's forgotten footnotes.

Of course, journals can be vain, too. I've caught myself writing as though a biographer were looking over my shoulder, polishing a sentence to make myself sound wiser than I was. But even that is revealing. The self we perform on the page is a clue to the self we hope to become.

Mostly, though, I think we write because we want to be paying attention. Life moves at a speed that doesn't invite reflection, and a notebook is a small act of resistance, a way of saying that this day happened, and I was here for it. The entries needn't be profound. "Rained all day. Made soup. Called Mom." Years later, those plain lines open like windows.

So the notebooks stay in their shoebox. I may never read them again, and that's all right. Their work was done in the writing, in the moment of turning the blur of living into something I could hold. Tonight, I'll probably add another page.
