**The Smaller Person**

At thirty-four, I stood in a Lisbon bakery rehearsing a sentence. I wanted a coffee and a custard tart. I had practiced on the walk over, but when the woman behind the counter looked up, the words scattered. I pointed and said "this" twice. She smiled kindly, the way you smile at a child, and I felt my face go hot.

That was the first thing learning Portuguese taught me: how much of my adult identity rested on sounding competent. At home I was someone people consulted. In that bakery I had the vocabulary of a toddler and the pride of a department head, and the two did not get along.

For months I was a smaller version of myself. I couldn't joke, argue, or offer the offhand observation that makes a person memorable. My personality, which I'd assumed was solid, turned out to depend heavily on grammar. What came through was simpler: patient, a little earnest, often confused. Strangely, people liked that person. My neighbor Dona Fernanda, who was seventy and had never left Portugal, began correcting me with great ceremony. "Not *eu sou cansado*," she'd say. "*Estou* cansado. You *are* tired, but you are not *made of* tired." I have never forgotten the difference between permanent and temporary states, and I think about it in English now too.

The second lesson was about listening. In my first language I listened lazily, already composing my reply. In Portuguese I had to catch every word like something thrown to me. I started noticing tone, gesture, and the pauses people left. I became a better listener in both languages, and I felt almost embarrassed by how much I'd been missing.

The third lesson was that mistakes are survivable. I once told a dinner table I was "very constipated" when I meant embarrassed. (The words are false friends, and I will say no more.) The table roared, someone refilled my glass, and I was more included than I'd been all week. Failing in public, I learned, mostly makes people warmer toward you. I had spent years avoiding the very thing that would have loosened me up.

Progress was invisible until it wasn't. For a long time, I felt stuck. Then one afternoon I realized I'd followed an entire conversation between two fast-talking taxi drivers and laughed at the right moment. No one gave me a certificate. A door I hadn't known was closed simply stood open.

I still make errors daily, and I still hear the slight gentleness people use with foreigners. But I no longer hear it as pity. I hear it as hospitality, extended to anyone willing to stand in front of strangers and be bad at something.

What I carry from those years is not fluency, exactly. It's a willingness to be the smaller person, to point and say "this" and try again. That person, it turns out, was never lesser. He was just learning, which is the one thing I'd stopped letting myself do.
