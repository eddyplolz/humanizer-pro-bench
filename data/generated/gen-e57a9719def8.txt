# History of the bicycle

The **history of the bicycle** spans more than two centuries, during which the vehicle evolved from a wooden, foot-propelled machine into the lightweight, efficient designs used worldwide today.

## Early precursors

The earliest widely recognized ancestor of the bicycle is the *Laufmaschine* ("running machine"), invented by German baron Karl von Drais and first demonstrated in 1817. Also called the draisine or "dandy horse," it consisted of two in-line wheels connected by a wooden frame. The rider sat astride and pushed against the ground with their feet. It had a steerable front wheel but no pedals. The device enjoyed brief popularity among the European upper classes but faded after a few years, partly because of poor road surfaces and bans on its use on sidewalks.

## Pedals and the velocipede

In the 1860s, French inventors, among them Pierre Michaux and Pierre Lallement, are credited with attaching cranks and pedals directly to the front wheel. The resulting "velocipede," nicknamed the "boneshaker" because of its rigid iron-and-wood construction and rough ride, sparked a short-lived craze across Europe and North America.

## High-wheelers

During the 1870s, designers sought greater speed by enlarging the front wheel, since each pedal revolution covered a distance proportional to its diameter. The result was the "penny-farthing," or high-wheel bicycle, popularized by manufacturers such as James Starley in Britain. Metal frames, solid rubber tires, and wire-spoked wheels improved performance, but the high seating position made falls dangerous, limiting its appeal largely to young, athletic men.

## The safety bicycle

The decisive advance came with the "safety bicycle." In 1885, John Kemp Starley introduced the Rover, featuring two wheels of similar size and a chain drive to the rear wheel. Its lower seat made it far more stable and accessible. The adoption of the pneumatic tire, developed by John Boyd Dunlop in 1888, greatly improved comfort and speed. By the 1890s, the "bicycle boom" had made cycling popular across social classes, and it played a notable role in the social mobility of women, who gained independence and influenced changes in clothing.

## Twentieth century and beyond

Improvements such as the freewheel, derailleur gears, and lighter alloys made bicycles more versatile during the early 1900s. Although the rise of the automobile reduced cycling's role in transportation in many wealthy countries, bicycles remained essential in much of Asia, Africa, and Europe. The 1970s saw a fitness-driven boom, and the invention of the mountain bike in California during that decade created a new category. Modern developments include carbon-fiber frames, electric-assist models, and bike-sharing systems, reflecting renewed interest in cycling as sustainable urban transport.
