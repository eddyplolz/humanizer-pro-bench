# Battle of Hastings

The **Battle of Hastings** was fought on 14 October 1066 between the Norman-French army of William, Duke of Normandy, and an English army under the Anglo-Saxon King Harold Godwinson. It took place approximately 7 miles (11 km) northwest of Hastings, close to the present-day town of Battle, East Sussex. The battle was a decisive Norman victory and marked the beginning of the Norman Conquest of England.

## Background

In January 1066, the childless King Edward the Confessor died, triggering a succession crisis. Harold Godwinson, the powerful Earl of Wessex, was crowned king almost immediately. However, William of Normandy claimed that Edward had promised him the throne and that Harold had sworn an oath to support his claim. Meanwhile, the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada also pressed a claim, invading northern England in September 1066.

Harold marched north and defeated Hardrada at the Battle of Stamford Bridge on 25 September. Only days later, William's invasion fleet landed at Pevensey on the Sussex coast on 28 September. Harold hurried his army south, covering roughly 250 miles in about two weeks.

## Battle

Harold's forces took up a defensive position on Senlac Hill, forming a shield wall along the ridge. The English army consisted largely of infantry, including elite housecarls and the less-equipped fyrd. William's army included infantry, archers, crossbowmen, and a significant cavalry contingent.

The fighting began in the morning, with Norman archers firing uphill with limited effect. Infantry and cavalry assaults followed but failed to break the shield wall. At one point, a rumour spread that William had been killed, and he reportedly lifted his helmet to show he was alive. Some Norman troops retreated, and some English soldiers pursued them down the hill, only to be cut down when the Normans turned on them. Whether the Normans used feigned retreats deliberately remains debated among historians.

As the day wore on, the English line weakened. Harold was killed late in the day, traditionally said to have been struck in the eye by an arrow, as depicted in the Bayeux Tapestry, although this is disputed. With their king dead, the English forces broke and fled.

## Aftermath

William continued to consolidate his position, receiving submission from English leaders and being crowned King of England on 25 December 1066 at Westminster Abbey. He later founded Battle Abbey on the site of the battle. The conquest brought profound changes to English society, language, law, and landholding.
