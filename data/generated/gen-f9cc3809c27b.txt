# Museum Returns 27 Looted Artifacts to Nigeria in Landmark Ceremony

**Collection of Benin bronzes and ceremonial objects to be displayed in Benin City after more than a century abroad**

*Hartwell, England* — A regional museum in northern England formally returned 27 artifacts to Nigeria on Tuesday. The objects were taken during a colonial-era military expedition in 1897, and the handover ended a decade of negotiations.

The Hartwell Museum of World Cultures transferred legal ownership of the collection to Nigeria's National Commission for Museums and Monuments during a ceremony attended by diplomats, museum staff, and members of the Edo diaspora community. The items include brass plaques, carved ivory pieces, and a ceremonial staff that once belonged to the royal court of the Kingdom of Benin.

"These are not simply objects," said Dr. Amara Okonkwo, the Nigerian delegation's lead representative. "They are records of our history, our artistry, and our identity. For generations, our communities have studied these works through photographs in foreign books. Today, that changes."

The artifacts were taken in 1897 when British forces attacked Benin City and removed thousands of items from the royal palace. Many were sold at auction or distributed among private collectors and institutions across Europe and North America. The museum acquired its pieces in 1934 through a bequest from the family of a former naval officer.

Museum director Helen Marsh said the decision followed extensive provenance research, which confirmed the objects' origins beyond reasonable doubt. "We had to ask ourselves what responsibility we have when we know how an object came to us," Marsh said. "The answer, ultimately, was clear. Holding onto them was no longer defensible."

The return comes amid growing pressure on Western institutions to address colonial-era acquisitions. In recent years, museums in Germany, the United States, and Scotland have returned portions of their Benin collections, while the British Museum continues to face calls for restitution of its much larger holdings.

Not everyone has welcomed the trend. Some curators argue that universal museums allow global audiences to encounter diverse cultures. Others have raised concerns about conservation capacity, though supporters of restitution note that Nigeria is building the Edo Museum of West African Art, a major facility designed by architect David Adjaye, to house returned works.

For Chidi Eze, a London-based university student of Edo descent who attended the ceremony, the moment was deeply personal. "My grandmother used to tell me stories about the palace and the bronzes," he said. "I never thought I would see any of this come home in my lifetime."

The artifacts will be flown to Lagos next week before traveling to Benin City, where they are expected to go on public display by early next year.
