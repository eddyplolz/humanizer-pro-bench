# The Slow Arithmetic of Walking

I did not set out to become a person who walks. It started because my car was in the shop for a week, and the bus route to my office required a transfer that added forty minutes. So one gray Tuesday I simply left the house on foot, grumbling, with the vague idea that I would figure something out.

What I figured out, somewhere around the third mile, was that I had not been alone with my thoughts in years.

Walking is a peculiar kind of attention. It's too slow to be about getting anywhere and too fast to be about standing still. Your body is occupied, but your mind is let off its leash. Problems I had been clenching my jaw over for weeks loosened on their own. I'd turn a corner and suddenly know how to word the difficult email, or realize that I'd been angry at my brother for the wrong reason. Nietzsche claimed that all truly great thoughts are conceived while walking. I'm not sure about the great ones, but the useful ones certainly seem to arrive that way.

When the car came back, I kept walking anyway. Mornings, mostly, before the day had made its demands. I began to know my neighborhood the way you know a person rather than a map. There was the man who watered his tomatoes in a faded bathrobe and raised one finger in greeting, like a conductor cueing a single violin. There was the corner where the smell of baking bread drifted out of a vent at precisely 6:40. There was a persimmon tree that I passed a hundred times before I noticed it was heavy with fruit, bright as lanterns against the bare branches. How much else, I wondered, had I driven past?

The habit has its own quiet discipline. Some days it's raining and I go anyway, and those turn out to be the best ones: the streets rinsed and empty, my own footsteps keeping time. Some days I'm tired or sad and the walk doesn't fix anything, but it lets me carry the sadness at a more manageable pace. Sorrow, I've learned, is easier to bear at three miles an hour.

I've also come to love the arithmetic of it. A mile is about two thousand steps. A good walk is a few thousand more. None of these steps feels like an accomplishment, and that's the point. Nobody applauds the single step. But they accumulate, like pennies in a jar, into something substantial: a year of mornings, a body that is stronger than it was, a mind that is a little less cluttered.

I think often now about how much of modern life is designed to spare us effort and, in sparing us, to spare us experience too. Walking refuses the bargain. It asks for your time and gives back something you didn't know you were missing: the texture of a place, the rhythm of your own breath, the odd peace of being exactly as fast as you are.

Tomorrow morning, I'll lace up my shoes again. I don't know what I'll think about. That's the whole idea.
