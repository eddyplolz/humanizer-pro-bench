Friendship used to feel smaller to me, but also closer. When I was younger, my idea of a friend was someone I saw in school, someone whose house I had been to, someone whose voice I knew without needing to check a screen. If I wanted to tell them something, I had to wait until lunch, or call their home phone and hope their parents did not answer. There was slowness in that kind of friendship, and sometimes frustration, but there was also a certain weight to it. Every interaction felt chosen. You had to make time.

Social media changed that completely. At first, it felt like a miracle. Suddenly friendship was not limited by distance, schedules, or even shyness. I could stay connected to people from old schools, summer programs, family trips, and eventually different stages of life. Someone I had not seen in years could still appear in my day through a photo, a joke, a birthday post, or a message sent after midnight. Social media made friendship seem continuous, as if no one ever had to fully disappear.

There is something beautiful about that. I have been able to witness the ordinary details of my friends’ lives in a way that would have been impossible before: their new apartments, bad haircuts, pets, travel photos, engagements, breakups, and quiet victories. I have celebrated people I might otherwise have lost. Sometimes a simple reaction to a story can reopen a door. A friendship that would have faded can survive through small digital gestures. Social media has made it easier to say, “I’m still here. I still know you.”

And yet, it has also changed friendship in ways that feel unsettling. It creates the illusion of intimacy without always requiring the work that intimacy demands. I can know what someone ate, where they vacationed, and what political opinion they posted, while having no real idea how they are actually doing. I can feel updated on a person’s life without ever asking them a sincere question. In that sense, social media can turn friendship into a kind of passive observation. We watch each other instead of truly meeting.

It has also blurred the meaning of the word “friend.” The number can rise into the hundreds, even thousands, but that abundance sometimes hides a different kind of loneliness. Being visible is not the same as being known. Getting likes is not the same as being comforted. Social media encourages performance, and friendship can become tangled up with presentation. Instead of sharing ourselves, we may begin curating ourselves, even for people who care about us. The pressure to seem happy, witty, successful, or unbothered can make honesty feel risky.

Still, I do not think social media has ruined friendship. I think it has revealed both its strengths and its weaknesses. It can help us maintain connection, but it cannot replace presence. It can open conversations, but it cannot guarantee depth. The friendships that matter most in my life are the ones that can survive both online and offline: the people I can message memes to, but also call in crisis; the people who notice when I go quiet; the people who know the version of me that exists beyond the profile.

Social media changed friendship by making it wider, faster, and more constant. But it also taught me that real friendship is still built the old-fashioned way: through attention, honesty, time, and care.
