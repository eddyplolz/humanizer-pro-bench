**Library of Alexandria**

The **Library of Alexandria** was one of the most famous libraries of the ancient world and a major center of scholarship in **Alexandria**, Egypt, during the **Hellenistic period**. It was associated with the **Mouseion** (“Temple of the Muses”), a research institution established under the **Ptolemaic dynasty** following the conquests of **Alexander the Great**. Although many details remain uncertain, the library has become a symbol of learning, intellectual ambition, and cultural loss.

The library was probably founded in the early 3rd century BCE, most likely during the reign of **Ptolemy I Soter** or his son **Ptolemy II Philadelphus**. The Ptolemies sought to make Alexandria the leading center of Greek culture and scholarship. To that end, they gathered texts from across the Mediterranean world, including works of literature, philosophy, science, mathematics, and history. Ancient sources describe energetic acquisition policies: books arriving in the city were reportedly copied, and in some cases the originals were kept for the library while copies were returned to their owners.

The Library of Alexandria was not merely a repository of scrolls but part of a wider scholarly community. Resident scholars studied, edited, and compared texts, helping to establish critical editions of major Greek authors such as **Homer**. The institution attracted prominent intellectuals, including **Euclid**, **Eratosthenes**, **Aristarchus of Samothrace**, and **Callimachus**. Callimachus is traditionally credited with compiling the **Pinakes**, a vast bibliographic catalogue often regarded as one of the earliest library catalogues in history.

Estimates of the library’s size vary widely, with ancient and modern figures ranging from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand scrolls. The exact organization of the library and the relationship between the main library and a possible “daughter library” in the **Serapeum** remain subjects of scholarly debate.

The destruction of the Library of Alexandria is one of the best-known episodes in cultural history, though it likely did not occur in a single catastrophic event. Ancient and later sources connect its decline to several episodes, including **Julius Caesar’s** campaign in Alexandria in 48 BCE, political turmoil in the Roman period, and the destruction of pagan institutions in late antiquity, especially during the 4th century CE. Modern historians generally conclude that the library diminished gradually over centuries rather than being suddenly annihilated.

The library’s legacy has endured far beyond antiquity. It has come to represent the ideal of universal knowledge and the fragility of cultural memory. In modern Alexandria, the **Bibliotheca Alexandrina**, inaugurated in 2002, was conceived as a tribute to the ancient institution and its enduring place in world history.
