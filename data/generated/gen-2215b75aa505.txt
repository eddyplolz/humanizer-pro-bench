**The Weight of a Hamburger**

When I was nine, I watched my uncle slaughter a pig. I had been told to stay in the truck, and of course I didn't. What I remember is not the blood but the quiet afterward, how ordinary the men became once it was done, rinsing their hands and talking about the weather. That night we ate pork shoulder, and I remember that it was delicious and that I could not stop thinking about the pig's eyes.

I've been circling that evening for most of my adult life.

For years I avoided the question the way you avoid a bill you suspect you can't pay. I ate chicken breasts that came in plastic, shaped like nothing that had ever lived. Then I read a book about industrial farming, one of those books whose purpose is to ruin your appetite, and it worked. I became a vegetarian for three years. I was sincere and a little insufferable about it.

What I believed then, and still believe, is that suffering matters. Pigs are intelligent and social, and they feel fear. If I accept that a dog's pain counts for something, I have to explain why a pig's doesn't, and "because it tastes good" is a poor argument. Most of the meat in the world comes from animals that lived in conditions I would call cruel if I saw them applied to anything I loved. That is hard to argue around, and I've stopped trying.

But the certainty I felt as a vegetarian has worn down, not because the arguments got weaker but because life got more complicated. I spent a winter with relatives whose diet was largely what they raised and hunted, and their relationship to meat was nothing like mine. They knew the animals, thanked them without embarrassment, and wasted nothing. I couldn't honestly say they were doing something worse than I was, standing in a grocery aisle buying almond milk shipped from a drought-stricken valley. Every way of eating costs something. The question is whether you can see the cost.

That, I think, is where I've landed, uncomfortably. The worst thing about modern meat is not that animals die but that the dying has been hidden from us. Distance makes cruelty easy. If I'm going to eat an animal, I should be willing to know where it lived and how it was killed. If I can't stand to know, that's information too.

So now I eat meat rarely and try to know its source. I buy from a farmer whose fields I've walked through. Sometimes I fail, and I eat the convenient thing at an airport or a wedding and feel the old pang. I don't think this makes me virtuous. It may only make me a more thoughtful participant in something I can't fully justify.

I used to want a clean answer, either a rule I could follow or a principle that would let me stop thinking. I've come to distrust that wish. Some questions are meant to stay slightly open, to keep nagging at you.

I still think about the pig sometimes. I don't think the memory is asking me to stop eating, exactly. It's asking me to stay awake while I do.
