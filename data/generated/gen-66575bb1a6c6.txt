**Trans-Siberian Railway**

The **Trans-Siberian Railway** is the longest railway line in the world, spanning across the Russian Federation from **Moscow** in the west to **Vladivostok** on the Pacific coast. Its main route covers about **9,289 kilometres** (5,772 miles), crossing a vast range of landscapes, climates, and time zones. The railway is one of the most significant transport networks in Eurasia and has played a major role in the economic, political, and cultural integration of Siberia and the Russian Far East.

Construction of the railway began in **1891** under the Russian Empire during the reign of **Tsar Alexander III**, with his son, the future **Nicholas II**, ceremonially initiating the project in Vladivostok. The railway was built in sections by thousands of labourers, soldiers, and convicts under difficult geographic and climatic conditions. By **1916**, a continuous rail connection from European Russia to the Pacific had been completed, aided by the construction of the **Amur Railway**, which allowed the route to remain entirely within Russian territory.

The Trans-Siberian Railway was conceived for both strategic and economic purposes. It enabled the imperial government to strengthen its control over distant eastern territories, encourage migration and settlement in Siberia, and promote trade and resource extraction. During the **Russian Civil War** and later in the **Soviet period**, the line was of immense military and logistical value. It continued to serve as a crucial artery for the movement of goods, people, and raw materials throughout the 20th century.

Several important branches and associated routes are often linked to the Trans-Siberian system. These include the **Trans-Mongolian Railway**, which runs from Ulan-Ude through Mongolia to Beijing, and the **Trans-Manchurian Railway**, which passes through northeastern China. Another major related line is the **Baikal–Amur Mainline** (BAM), built farther north in the Soviet era as an alternative to the main route.

The railway crosses **eight time zones** and serves many major cities, including **Yekaterinburg**, **Omsk**, **Novosibirsk**, **Krasnoyarsk**, **Irkutsk**, **Ulan-Ude**, **Khabarovsk**, and Vladivostok. It also passes near **Lake Baikal**, the world’s deepest freshwater lake. Over time, much of the line has been **electrified**, increasing efficiency and capacity.

Beyond its practical importance, the Trans-Siberian Railway has acquired symbolic and cultural significance. It is often associated with the vastness of Russia and has featured prominently in literature, journalism, film, and tourism. Long-distance passenger journeys on the line are internationally known for their scale and for the experience of crossing the Eurasian continent by rail.

Today, the Trans-Siberian Railway remains a key component of Russia’s transport infrastructure and an enduring emblem of one of the largest railway enterprises ever undertaken.
