**The Weight of Paper**

After my grandmother died, I found her letters in a shoebox, rubber-banded by year. Mine were in there too, dutiful childhood notes about school and a lost tooth, which she had kept for decades. But it was her side of the correspondence I couldn't stop reading, the letters my mother had saved and passed back to me.

Nothing happens in them. The tomatoes are coming in late. Mrs. Dolan's hip is no better. It rained Tuesday, then Wednesday, then, she wrote, "it seemed to lose interest." I laughed out loud at that, alone in my kitchen, hearing her dry voice come through the slanted blue ink. She had crossed out a word and written another above it, and I could see her hesitate and decide. That small correction told me more about her than any photograph.

I think this is what we lose when we stop writing letters: not information, but evidence of attention. A letter is proof that someone sat down and held you in mind for twenty uninterrupted minutes. They didn't do it while waiting for coffee or half-watching television. They chose a page, a pen, and you. Now I send dozens of messages a day, and they are quick and warm and useful, but they are also weightless. They arrive mid-distraction and are answered mid-distraction. Almost none of them are kept. Who could keep them?

Letters also required a kind of patience that shaped what people said. You couldn't fire off a feeling and retract it ten seconds later. You had to let a thought finish itself, and sometimes in the finishing it changed. My grandfather, I learned from the shoebox, once wrote three pages to my grandmother about a quarrel, and the last paragraph contradicts the first. He hadn't meant to forgive her when he began. The letter got him there. A text would have kept him angry, or ended the argument in a single clipped word.

And then there was the waiting. We treat waiting as pure cost now, a flaw to be engineered away. But the days between sending and receiving gave longing somewhere to live. You walked to the mailbox with a small, private hope. When something came, it had traveled to reach you. It had a stamp, a smudge, a faint smell of someone else's house.

Last winter I wrote a letter to an old friend who had moved across the country. It took me an hour and a half, far longer than I expected, because I kept discovering things I hadn't realized I wanted to say. My handwriting had gone ragged from disuse. I crossed out a word and wrote another above it. When I dropped the envelope in the box, I felt oddly exposed, as though I'd handed over something I couldn't edit.

She wrote back three weeks later. I have it still, on my desk, the paper slightly creased where her hand pressed down. It says, among other things, that it has been raining, and that the rain seems to have lost interest.
