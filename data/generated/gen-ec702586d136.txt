# Last Aisle: Harlan Falls Loses Its Only Grocery Store

**HARLAN FALLS —** The automatic doors at Miller's Market slid shut for the final time Saturday evening, leaving this town of 2,100 without a grocery store for the first time in 71 years.

Owner Dale Miller, 64, flipped the sign to "Closed" at 6 p.m. as roughly three dozen residents stood in the parking lot. Some brought flowers. Others simply stood in silence.

"I tried everything I could think of," Miller said, his voice catching. "The numbers just stopped working."

Miller inherited the store from his father in 1994. He said rising wholesale costs, higher energy bills, and competition from a discount supercenter 28 miles away steadily drained his margins. When his refrigeration system failed in March, the $85,000 replacement estimate settled the question.

"You can't ask a store this size to absorb that," he said. "I'd have been working just to pay off a freezer."

The closure hits hardest for residents without reliable transportation. Nearly one in five Harlan Falls households includes someone over 65, according to county census data, and many no longer drive.

"I'm 82. I'm not driving to Dunmore on the highway," said Ruth Eckhart, who has shopped at Miller's weekly since the 1960s. "My son says he'll take me every other Saturday, but what do I eat in between?"

Mayor Connie Reyes said the town council will hold an emergency meeting Thursday to discuss options, including a volunteer delivery program through the senior center and a possible shuttle service to the supercenter. She also said she has contacted the state agriculture department about grants for rural food access.

"A grocery store isn't just a business," Reyes said. "It's where you find out who's in the hospital, who needs help, whose kid just got into college. We're going to lose that, too."

Local convenience options remain limited. The Gas-N-Go on Route 9 stocks milk, bread, and canned goods, but at prices Miller's customers say run 30 to 40 percent higher. The town's weekly farmers market, which operates only from June through September, offers produce but not staples.

Some residents are exploring a community-owned cooperative store, a model that has revived groceries in other rural towns. A planning group is scheduled to meet at the library next week.

Teenager Marcus Webb, who worked the register at Miller's for two years, lingered after the crowd dispersed. "It was my first job," he said. "Everybody knew my name."

Miller said he has no immediate plans beyond cleaning out the building. The property is listed for sale.

"I just hope somebody builds something here," he said, looking back at the dark windows. "This town deserves it."
