**Axolotl**

The **axolotl** (*Ambystoma mexicanum*) is a species of salamander native to the lake complex underlying the Valley of Mexico, most notably the remnants of **Lake Xochimilco** near Mexico City. It is one of the most widely recognized amphibians in the world because of its unusual appearance, aquatic lifestyle, and remarkable ability to regenerate body parts. Unlike many other salamanders, the axolotl typically retains its larval features throughout life, a condition known as **neoteny**.

Axolotls have a broad head, lidless eyes, a laterally flattened tail, and three pairs of feathery external gills projecting from the sides of the head. Wild individuals are usually dark brown or black with mottled markings, while captive-bred varieties may be leucistic, albino, golden, or melanoid. Adults commonly reach lengths of 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in), though size varies.

The species is unusual among amphibians in that it normally remains fully aquatic and does not undergo complete metamorphosis into a terrestrial adult form. Although metamorphosis can occur rarely in nature or be induced artificially by hormones, most axolotls retain gills and other juvenile characteristics while becoming sexually mature. They feed on worms, insect larvae, crustaceans, mollusks, and small fish, using suction to capture prey.

Axolotls are especially noted for their extraordinary regenerative capacities. They can regrow limbs, tails, parts of the spinal cord, skin, and even portions of the heart and brain without extensive scarring. For this reason, they have become important model organisms in biological and medical research, particularly in studies of regeneration, development, and genetics. They have also been used extensively in laboratories because they breed readily in captivity.

Historically, axolotls were common in the interconnected lakes of the Mexican Basin and held cultural significance in Aztec mythology. The name “axolotl” is derived from the Nahuatl word *āxōlōtl*, often associated with the deity **Xolotl**. In modern times, however, the species has suffered a severe decline in the wild. Urbanization, water pollution, habitat loss, drainage of lakes, and the introduction of invasive fish such as tilapia and carp have greatly reduced its natural population.

The axolotl is classified as **Critically Endangered** on the IUCN Red List. Conservation efforts have focused on habitat restoration in Xochimilco, captive breeding programs, and the creation of refuges free of invasive species. Despite its precarious status in the wild, the axolotl remains abundant in captivity and is popular in the pet trade and public aquaria.

Because of its distinctive biology and conservation status, the axolotl has become both a scientific icon and a symbol of the ecological challenges facing freshwater habitats in central Mexico.
