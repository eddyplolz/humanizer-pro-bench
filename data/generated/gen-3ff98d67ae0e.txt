**Antikythera mechanism**

The **Antikythera mechanism** is an ancient Greek hand-powered mechanical device, widely regarded as the earliest known example of an analog computer. It was designed to calculate and display astronomical phenomena, including the positions of the Sun and Moon, lunar phases, eclipse cycles, and possibly the motions of some planets. The device is generally dated to the late 2nd or early 1st century BCE.

The mechanism was discovered in 1901 among the remains of a shipwreck off the Greek island of **Antikythera**, between Crete and the Peloponnese. Recovered as a set of heavily corroded bronze fragments, it initially attracted limited attention. Its extraordinary complexity became clear only decades later, when researchers identified finely cut gears embedded within the fragments. Modern imaging techniques, especially X-ray computed tomography, have since revealed much of its internal structure and inscriptions.

The device was housed in a wooden case roughly the size of a shoebox. On its front face were circular dials with pointers indicating astronomical information, probably including the zodiac and the Egyptian calendar. A hand crank was likely used to drive a system of more than 30 bronze gears. These gears translated rotational motion into mathematical relationships corresponding to celestial cycles. The mechanism modeled the irregular motion of the Moon with remarkable sophistication, incorporating a pin-and-slot arrangement that simulated variations in lunar speed consistent with ancient Greek astronomical theory.

The back of the mechanism contained spiral dials used to track longer-term cycles. One dial represented the **Metonic cycle** of 19 years, which reconciles lunar months with the solar year. Another tracked the **Saros cycle**, used to predict eclipses. A subsidiary dial appears to have marked the **Exeligmos**, a longer eclipse cycle improving prediction accuracy. Inscriptions on the device served as a kind of operating manual, explaining the function of the dials and astronomical displays.

The Antikythera mechanism demonstrates a level of technical skill once thought impossible for the Hellenistic world. Its precision gearing suggests a sophisticated tradition of instrument-making, though no other surviving device of comparable complexity from antiquity is known. Scholars have linked it to Greek scientific traditions associated with figures such as Hipparchus, Posidonius, and Archimedes, though its exact origin and maker remain uncertain.

The mechanism has transformed modern understanding of ancient science and technology. It shows that Greek craftsmen and astronomers could embody advanced mathematical knowledge in intricate mechanical form. Often described as “centuries ahead of its time,” the Antikythera mechanism remains one of the most celebrated archaeological discoveries in the history of science.
