# Atacama Desert

The **Atacama Desert** (Spanish: *Desierto de Atacama*) is a desert plateau on the Pacific coast of western South America, covering a strip of land roughly 1,600 km (1,000 mi) long, mostly in northern Chile. It is widely regarded as the driest non-polar desert in the world and is one of the oldest continuously arid regions on Earth.

## Geography

The desert lies between the Pacific Ocean and the Andes Mountains, extending from the southern border of Peru to around La Serena in Chile. It covers approximately 105,000 km² (41,000 sq mi), though broader definitions that include adjacent arid regions give a larger area. The terrain includes salt flats (*salares*), sand dunes, rocky plains, and volcanic peaks. The Salar de Atacama, the largest salt flat in Chile, lies in the east of the region.

## Climate

The extreme aridity results from a combination of factors. The Andes block moisture from the Amazon Basin to the east, while the cold Humboldt Current and a persistent subtropical high-pressure system suppress rainfall from the Pacific. Some weather stations in the interior have never recorded rainfall, and the average annual precipitation in many areas is under 15 mm (0.6 in). Evidence suggests that some parts of the desert have experienced hyperarid conditions for millions of years.

Despite the dryness, coastal areas experience a persistent fog known as *camanchaca*, which provides moisture for some plant and animal life.

## Ecology

Vegetation is sparse, but the desert supports specialized organisms, including cacti, lichens, and hardy shrubs. In rare years of heavy rainfall, often linked to El Niño, parts of the desert bloom in a phenomenon called *desierto florido* ("flowering desert"). Fauna includes vicuñas, guanacos, foxes, flamingos in the salt lakes, and numerous reptiles and insects. Researchers have also studied microbial life in the soils, which are sometimes compared to those of Mars.

## History and economy

Indigenous peoples, including the Atacameño (Lickanantay) and Chinchorro cultures, inhabited the region for thousands of years. In the 19th century, the desert became economically important for its deposits of nitrate (saltpeter) and guano, which were crucial for fertilizer production. Control over these resources contributed to the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), in which Chile gained territory from Bolivia and Peru.

Today, mining dominates the economy, particularly copper and lithium. The Chuquicamata mine is among the largest open-pit copper mines in the world.

## Astronomy and science

The desert's high altitude, dry air, and minimal light pollution make it one of the best places on Earth for astronomical observation. Facilities include the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) and the Paranal Observatory, home to the Very Large Telescope.

## Tourism

Popular destinations include San Pedro de Atacama, the Valle de la Luna, the El Tatio geysers, and numerous high-altitude lagoons.
