# Heat Wave Pushes State Power Grid to the Brink as Residents Urged to Conserve

**Officials warn of rolling outages as record demand strains aging infrastructure**

A punishing heat wave that has gripped the region for six straight days pushed the state's power grid to its limits Thursday, prompting emergency conservation appeals and raising fears of rolling blackouts as temperatures climbed past 108 degrees in several cities.

The state's grid operator issued its highest-level alert at 4:15 p.m., after electricity demand hit an all-time peak of 52,300 megawatts, surpassing the previous record set in 2022. Reserves fell to dangerously low levels during the early evening hours, when solar output declines but air conditioners continue to run at full capacity.

"We are in a very tight situation," said Dana Whitfield, a spokesperson for the grid operator. "Every household and business that can reduce usage between 4 and 9 p.m. is helping keep the lights on for everyone."

The governor declared a state of emergency Wednesday, which allows regulators to temporarily relax some emissions limits so backup generators can run longer. Public buildings have been asked to dim lights and raise thermostats to 78 degrees.

Utilities reported scattered outages affecting roughly 41,000 customers, mostly caused by transformers overheating in neighborhoods where equipment has been running at maximum load for days. Crews worked through the afternoon to restore service, though some residents remained without power for more than six hours.

In the capital, the city opened 14 cooling centers, including libraries and community halls. At one center downtown, retiree Marcus Delgado said he came after his apartment hit 94 degrees inside. "My building's air conditioning gave out around noon," he said. "I'm just grateful there's somewhere to go."

Health officials reported a sharp increase in heat-related emergency room visits, with hospitals treating more than 300 patients for heat exhaustion and dehydration since Monday. Authorities urged residents to check on elderly neighbors and avoid outdoor activity during afternoon hours.

Energy analysts say the crisis highlights long-standing vulnerabilities. Much of the state's transmission infrastructure was built decades ago, and several large power plants have been retired in recent years faster than replacement capacity, including battery storage, has come online.

"Extreme heat is no longer an occasional stress test. It's becoming the norm," said Priya Raman, an energy policy professor at the state university. "The grid was designed for a different climate."

Meteorologists forecast little relief before the weekend, with an excessive heat warning in effect through Sunday night. Grid officials said they have secured emergency power imports from neighboring states but cautioned that those supplies are also tight, as the heat wave extends across much of the region.

Residents are encouraged to delay running dishwashers and laundry, close blinds during the day, and pre-cool homes in the morning.
