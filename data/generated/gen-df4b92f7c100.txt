**The Fifth Day**

For six years I spent Sunday evenings in a low hum of dread. Nothing was wrong, exactly. The job was fine, the colleagues were kind. But the weekend had a way of evaporating. Saturday went to errands and recovery, Sunday to anticipating Monday. I was always living in the margins of my own life.

Then my company ran a pilot: four days a week, same pay, same expectations of output. I assumed it was a trick. I figured I'd either work ten-hour days or come back to a mountain of Friday's undone tasks.

What actually happened was stranger. The work shrank to fit the time. Meetings that had wandered for an hour found their point in twenty minutes. We stopped scheduling gatherings to discuss other gatherings. When you only have four days, you protect them. People started saying no more often, and, oddly, everything important still got done.

The research since has echoed what I felt. In the 2022 UK pilot, which involved about 61 companies and roughly 2,900 workers, the large majority of firms kept the shorter week afterward. Employees reported less burnout and better sleep, and revenue held steady. Iceland's earlier trials told a similar story. This isn't proof that every workplace could do it, but it undercuts the assumption that more hours automatically means more value.

That assumption is the real problem. We inherited the five-day week from an industrial era when output tracked hours on the factory floor. Most of us no longer work that way. Knowledge work runs on attention, and attention doesn't scale linearly. The fifth day was often the one where I was physically present but mentally gone, answering emails to prove I existed.

What I got back was not just rest, though rest mattered. It was ordinary life. I went to the dentist without negotiating a calendar. I cooked a real dinner on a Friday. I spent a long afternoon with my father, who is older than he used to be, and I noticed how much of our relationship had been conducted in scraps of leftover time. A day off isn't a luxury so much as a place for the things that make a person more than an employee.

Critics worry about fairness, and they're right to. A four-day week is harder to arrange for nurses, teachers, and restaurant staff, whose work can't be compressed by cutting meetings. But that's an argument for designing it thoughtfully, with staggered shifts, more hiring, and honest measurement, not for refusing to try it where it works. Some people will also compress five days into four longer ones, which misses the point. The goal isn't the same hours in fewer days. It's fewer hours, spent well.

I don't think the four-day week will fix everything. Bad managers will remain bad. Pressure will find new outlets. But I came back from that pilot with a clearer sense of what work is for. It's a part of life, not its organizing principle.

These days, Sunday evenings are quiet. I notice that more than any productivity statistic.
