# Trans-Siberian Railway

The **Trans-Siberian Railway** (Russian: Транссибирская магистраль, *Transsibirskaya magistral*) is a network of railway lines connecting European Russia with the Russian Far East. Spanning roughly 9,289 kilometres (5,772 miles) between Moscow and Vladivostok, it is the longest continuous railway line in the world. The route crosses eight time zones and passes through more than 80 cities and towns.

## History

Construction began in 1891 under Tsar Alexander III, with Tsarevich Nicholas (later Nicholas II) ceremonially inaugurating work at Vladivostok. The project was driven by a desire to consolidate Russian control over its eastern territories, promote settlement and trade, and strengthen military mobility in the Far East. Finance minister Sergei Witte was a key advocate and organizer.

Thousands of laborers, including soldiers, convicts, and hired workers, built the line, contending with permafrost, difficult terrain, harsh winters, and vast distances. The main sections were largely complete by 1904, although the Circum-Baikal section around Lake Baikal was finished later, and passengers initially crossed the lake by ferry. The railway's usefulness was tested during the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), when its limited capacity hampered Russian supply efforts.

## Route

The principal route runs from Moscow eastward through Nizhny Novgorod, Yekaterinburg, Omsk, Novosibirsk, Krasnoyarsk, Irkutsk, Ulan-Ude, Chita, and Khabarovsk before reaching Vladivostok on the Pacific coast. It crosses major geographic features including the Ural Mountains, the Ob and Yenisei rivers, and the shores of Lake Baikal, the world's deepest lake.

Several branches extend from the main line. The Trans-Mongolian route diverges near Ulan-Ude and travels through Mongolia to Beijing, while the Trans-Manchurian route enters northeastern China via Harbin. The later Baikal–Amur Mainline (BAM) provides a northern alternative through eastern Siberia.

## Operation and significance

The railway is operated by Russian Railways and is fully electrified and double-tracked. It carries a substantial share of Russia's freight, including coal, timber, oil products, and containers moving between Asia and Europe. It plays an important role in transcontinental trade, and the volume of container traffic has made it a competitor to maritime shipping routes for certain goods.

Passenger service continues to operate, with the Moscow–Vladivostok journey taking about six to seven days on the long-distance train *Rossiya*. The railway has also become a popular tourist attraction, drawing travelers interested in experiencing Siberia's landscapes and the culture of long-distance rail travel.

## Cultural impact

The Trans-Siberian has featured widely in literature, film, and travel writing, symbolizing both the immensity of Russia and the romance of overland journeys. It remains a defining feature of Russian infrastructure and national identity.
