# Microplastics Found in Most Tap and Bottled Water Samples, New Study Reports

*Researchers say filtration methods could cut particle levels significantly, but health effects remain unclear*

A new multi-country study has found microplastic particles in the large majority of drinking water samples tested, renewing questions about how these tiny fragments move through the water supply and what they might mean for human health.

The research, published this week in the journal *Environmental Science Advances*, analyzed more than 1,200 samples of tap water, bottled water, and household-filtered water from 14 countries. Microplastics were detected in 87 percent of samples. Bottled water showed the highest average concentration, at roughly twice the level found in municipal tap water.

"We expected to find them, but we didn't expect them to be so consistent across regions and water sources," said Dr. Elena Marchetti, the study's lead author. "Microplastics are now part of the background of our water systems."

## Where the Particles Come From

The team used spectroscopy to identify particle types and found that polyethylene, polypropylene, and polyester fibers were the most common. Researchers say likely sources include degraded packaging, synthetic textiles shed during washing, tire dust, and aging plumbing materials. In bottled water, they linked many particles to the bottles and caps themselves, which can shed fragments through repeated opening or exposure to heat.

The study also looked at particles smaller than one micrometer, often called nanoplastics, which are harder to detect and may be more biologically active. Although these were measured with less certainty, the authors estimate they may outnumber larger fragments by a wide margin.

## What Helps

Not all the findings were discouraging. Water treated with reverse osmosis or multi-stage carbon block filters had particle counts about 70 to 90 percent lower than unfiltered samples. Simple boiling and filtering through a fine mesh also reduced particle levels in some tests, though results varied.

## Health Questions Remain

The authors stressed that the study did not assess health outcomes. Scientists still don't know what level of microplastic exposure, if any, poses a risk. Some laboratory studies suggest the particles could trigger inflammation or carry harmful chemicals, but evidence in humans is limited.

"People shouldn't panic, but this isn't something to dismiss either," said Dr. Raj Patel, an environmental toxicologist not involved in the research. "We need standardized testing methods and long-term studies to understand what these exposures mean."

The authors are calling for international monitoring standards and greater investment in treatment technology. Several regulators have begun reviewing guidelines, though no binding limits for microplastics in drinking water currently exist in most countries.
