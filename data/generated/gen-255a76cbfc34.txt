City Council Approves New Bike-Lane Network After Heated, Split Vote

After months of public debate and a tense meeting that stretched late into the evening, the City Council on Tuesday approved a sweeping new bike-lane network designed to expand cycling access across the city. The plan passed by a narrow 5-4 vote, reflecting sharp divisions among elected officials and residents over cost, traffic impacts and public safety.

The approved proposal calls for roughly 22 miles of new protected bike lanes, along with upgrades to key intersections, improved signage and expanded bike parking near business districts, schools and transit hubs. City transportation officials said the network is intended to connect neighborhoods that currently lack safe cycling infrastructure and to create continuous routes linking downtown with the north, west and riverfront areas.

Supporters hailed the vote as a major step toward reducing traffic congestion, lowering emissions and making streets safer for cyclists and pedestrians alike.

“This is about giving people more options for how they move around the city,” Council President Maria Alvarez said after the vote. “For years, residents have asked for a transportation system that is safer, more modern and less dependent on cars. Tonight, we acted on that.”

But the decision came only after hours of contentious testimony from residents, business owners and advocacy groups. Opponents argued the project would eliminate too many on-street parking spaces and worsen traffic on already crowded corridors. Several business owners warned that construction could disrupt commerce and reduce customer access.

“This plan moves too fast and asks neighborhoods to shoulder too much of the burden,” said Councilmember James Porter, who voted against the measure. “We all want safer streets, but this proposal needed more revision and stronger protections for local businesses.”

The debate inside council chambers often mirrored the broader divide in the community. Cycling advocates packed the room wearing reflective stickers and ringing bicycle bells after the final vote, while critics left frustrated, saying the council had ignored practical concerns in favor of a political statement.

Under the plan, construction will begin early next year in three phases, with priority given to corridors that city data identified as having higher rates of bicycle and pedestrian crashes. Officials said funding will come from a mix of local capital funds, state transportation grants and federal infrastructure dollars.

Mayor Elaine Brooks, who has strongly backed the initiative, said she would sign the measure later this week. In a statement, she called the network “an investment in safety, mobility and the city’s future.”

Still, with opponents already calling for route adjustments and possible legal challenges tied to environmental and traffic review, the political battle may be far from over. For now, however, supporters are celebrating a hard-fought victory that could reshape how residents travel through the city for years to come.
