**Svalbard Global Seed Vault**

The **Svalbard Global Seed Vault** is a secure seed storage facility located on the Norwegian island of **Spitsbergen** in the **Svalbard archipelago**, about 1,300 kilometres from the North Pole. Opened in **2008**, it was established to preserve duplicate samples of seeds from gene banks around the world, providing a safeguard against the loss of crop diversity caused by war, natural disasters, equipment failure, climate change, or poor management.

The Seed Vault is built into the side of **Platåberget**, a sandstone mountain near the settlement of **Longyearbyen**. Its remote Arctic location was chosen for both political and environmental reasons. Svalbard is geologically stable, and its naturally cold climate, combined with surrounding permafrost, helps protect the seeds even in the event of a power failure. The facility stores seeds at approximately **−18 °C (0 °F)**, a standard temperature for long-term conservation in seed banks.

Sometimes referred to as the world’s “**doomsday vault**,” the Svalbard Global Seed Vault functions as a backup repository rather than a conventional seed bank. It does not own the seeds deposited there; instead, seeds remain the property of the depositing institutions under a “black box” arrangement, meaning only depositors retain the right to access their collections. This system is intended to support national, regional, and international gene banks by offering an additional layer of security.

The vault was developed by the **Government of Norway** in partnership with the **Nordic Genetic Resource Center (NordGen)** and the **Crop Trust**. It has the capacity to store millions of seed samples, representing a vast range of food crops and their wild relatives. Collections stored there include varieties of wheat, rice, maize, barley, beans, and many other agriculturally important species. The preservation of such genetic diversity is considered essential for future plant breeding, food security, and adaptation to changing environmental conditions.

The Seed Vault has also played a practical role in conservation. In **2015**, the **International Center for Agricultural Research in the Dry Areas (ICARDA)** became the first institution to withdraw seeds from the vault after conflict in Syria disrupted operations at its regional gene bank. The withdrawn seeds were used to regenerate collections and later redeposit fresh samples, demonstrating the vault’s purpose as a functioning global insurance policy for agriculture.

Since its opening, the Svalbard Global Seed Vault has become an international symbol of efforts to preserve biodiversity and secure the genetic foundations of the world’s food supply. It is widely regarded as one of the most important facilities for the long-term conservation of crop diversity.
